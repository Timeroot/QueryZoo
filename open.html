Is $\mathrm{D}(f) = \widetilde O(\mathrm{R}(f)^2)$? total #

Gap between exponents 2 and 3. Both neighbouring error models are settled at 2.

How much can randomness save a decision tree on a total function? Nisan's simulation gives $\mathrm{D}(f)\le 27\,\mathrm{R}(f)^3$; the pointer functions give a family with $\mathrm{D}=\widetilde\Omega(\mathrm{R}^2)$. Nobody has closed either end since.

Best known

  • \(\mathrm{D}(f) \le 27\,\mathrm{R}(f)^3\)
    Nisan91 BdW02
    Through the combinatorial core: $\mathrm{D}\le\mathrm{C}^1\mathrm{bs}\le\mathrm{bs}^3$ and $\mathrm{bs}\le 3\mathrm{R}$. Every factor of the cube is a step nobody knows how to skip.
  • \(\mathrm{D}(f_n) = \widetilde\Omega\big(\mathrm{R}(f_n)^2\big)\)
    witness: \(g_{\mathrm{ABBLSS}}\)ABBLSS17
    The best separation, and it costs polylogs. Before the pointer functions the record was the $\mathrm{D}=\Theta(\mathrm{R}_0^{1.33})$ of \(\bar{\wedge}\text{-tree}\), which Saks and Wigderson conjectured to be optimal.
  • \(\mathrm{D}(f) = O(\mathrm{R}_0(f)^2) \text{ and } \mathrm{D}(f) = O(\mathrm{R}^1(f)^2)\)
    Nisan91 ABBLSS17
    Both are tight up to polylogs, by the same pointer functions. So zero error and one-sided error are settled at exponent 2, and two-sided bounded error is the only one of the three left open -- which is why a quadratic answer is the popular guess.

Why it matters

This is the headline cell of the classical corner, and it is the bottleneck in several others: $\mathrm{D}$ vs $\mathrm{bs}$, $\mathrm{D}$ vs $\deg$ and $\mathrm{R}$ vs $\mathrm{Q}$ all run through the same cubic-vs-quadratic slack. It is also the cell that says what randomness is worth on a total function: quadratic would mean a coin never buys more than the square root of the work, which is exactly what the Las Vegas and one-sided cases already say.

Is $\mathrm{D}(f) = O(\mathrm{bs}(f)^2)$? total #

Gap between exponents 2 and 3.

Block sensitivity is one of the two workhorse lower bounds on $\mathrm{D}$. We know $\mathrm{D}(f)\le\mathrm{s}(f)\mathrm{bs}(f)^2\le\mathrm{bs}(f)^3$ [BdW02, Cor. 1] and that $\mathrm{D}(f)$ can be $\Omega(\mathrm{bs}(f)^2)$. Which is the truth?

Best known

    Rubinstein95 BdW02
    Open problem 2 of [BdW02]; still open.

Why it matters

$\mathrm{bs}$ sits between $\mathrm{s}$ and $\mathrm{C}$, so this is the cleanest remaining question in the classical combinatorial core.

What is the optimal exponent of $\mathrm{bs}$ versus $\mathrm{s}$? total #

Quadratic conjecture refuted; exponent gap [2.1308255793978239…, 4] (source checked October 22, 2026).

The proposed quadratic upper bound is false. [OpenAISensitivity26] first constructed total functions with a fixed exponent strictly larger than two. The accepted public theorem [Prove2MeSensitivity26] gives, for every $\varepsilon>0$ and arbitrarily large sensitivity, $$\mathrm{bs}(f)\ge\mathrm{s}(f)^{\alpha-\varepsilon},\qquad \alpha=\frac{\log(14055^8)}{\log(3738277295413674)}=2.1308255793978239\ldots.$$ The universal upper bound remains $\mathrm{bs}\le2\deg^2\le2\mathrm{s}^4$. What is the optimal exponent in this interval?

Best known

  • \(\mathrm{bs}(f) \le 2\deg(f)^2 \le 2\,\mathrm{s}(f)^4\)
    NS94 Huang19
  • \(\forall\varepsilon>0,\ S\ge2,\ \exists f:\ \mathrm{s}(f)\ge S,\ \mathrm{bs}(f)\ge\mathrm{s}(f)^{\alpha-\varepsilon}\)
    Prove2MeSensitivity26
    $\alpha=\log(14055^8)/\log(3738277295413674)$. The public theorem is marked Proved. No assertion of a loss-free endpoint or a matching upper bound.
  • \(\exists\alpha>2,\ (F_m):\ \mathrm{bs}(F_m,0)\ge\mathrm{s}(F_m)^\alpha,\quad \mathrm{bs}(F_m,0)\to\infty\)
    OpenAISensitivity26
    Corollary 4.3 amplifies a fixed nested-tournament seed.
  • \(\mathrm{bs}(f) \ge \tfrac23 \mathrm{s}(f)^2 - \tfrac13\mathrm{s}(f)\)
    AmbainisSun11
    Improving Rubinstein's $\mathrm{s}^2/2$.

Why it matters

Huang settled polynomial boundedness, and the 2026 constructions rule out its quadratic strengthening. Improving either endpoint remains open. Since $\mathrm{bs}\le\mathrm{fbs}=\Theta(\mathrm{RC})$ and $\lambda\le\mathrm{s}$, the same family improves the lower exponents for fractional block sensitivity and randomized certificates versus sensitivity, and block sensitivity versus spectral sensitivity. The larger existing cubic separations of $\mathrm{D}$ and $\mathrm{C}$ from sensitivity are not improved by this implication.

What is the exponent of $\mathrm{D}$ versus $\mathrm{s}$? total #

Gap between exponents 3 and 6 -- the widest gap left in the total-function matrix.

Huang's theorem is what makes this cell finite at all: $\deg\le\mathrm{s}^2$, and Midrijanis' $\mathrm{D}\le\mathrm{bs}\cdot\deg\le2\deg^3$ then gives $\mathrm{D}(f)\le 2\,\mathrm{s}(f)^6$. The widest separation anyone has built is cubic. Nothing is known to rule out either end.

Best known

  • \(\mathrm{D}(f) \le \mathrm{bs}(f)\deg(f) \le 2\deg(f)^3 \le 2\,\mathrm{s}(f)^6\)
    Midrijanis04 NS94 Huang19
    Two steps, and the second one is tight: $\deg\le\mathrm{s}^2$ is matched by \(\mathrm{AND}_n \circ \mathrm{OR}_n\), which has $\deg = n^2$ against $\mathrm{s}=n$. So this route cannot do better than the square of whatever the truth is for $\mathrm{D}$ versus $\deg$, and the cell is that open problem wearing a different hat.
  • \(\mathrm{D}(f_n) = \widetilde\Omega\big(\mathrm{s}(f_n)^3\big)\)
    witness: \(f_{\mathrm{BHT}}\)BHT17
    Desensitize a function with cheap unambiguous certificates -- triple every bit and accept only $ppp$ for $p$ a $1$-certificate, which drives $\mathrm{s}_0$ down to $1$ without moving anything else -- then compose on the outside with a function whose certificates are almost all ones. Sensitivity stays $\widetilde O(n)$ while $\mathrm{Q}$, and so $\mathrm{D}$, multiplies up to $\widetilde\Omega(n^3)$.
  • \(\mathrm{D}(f) = O(\deg(f)^2)\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathrm{D}(f) = O(\mathrm{s}(f)^4)\)
    CPS23
    Which is why $4$, not $3$, is the natural guess for the answer -- though no function is known that reaches it.
  • \(\mathrm{D}(P) = O(\mathrm{s}(P)^2) \text{ for every graph property } P\)
    Turan84 CPS23
    Turán''s $\mathrm{s}(P) = \Omega(n)$ for non-trivial graph properties, against the trivial $\mathrm{D}(P)\le\binom n2$. [CPS23] prove the matching $\mathrm{D}(P)=O(\deg(P)^2)$ and get quadratic bounds for two further classes: functions with a constant number of alternations, and "zebra" functions, where every monotone path from $0^n$ to $1^n$ alternates equally often (this contains the symmetric and the monotone functions).

Why it matters

This is the cell every other row inherits: a bound in terms of $\mathrm{s}$ is almost always routed through $\deg$ or $\mathrm{bs}$, so the sixth power reappears all over the $\mathrm{s}$ column. It is also the one place where Huang's theorem is load-bearing -- before 2019 no polynomial bound existed here at all.

A useful contrast is \(\lambda(f)\), the spectral sensitivity that Huang's proof actually bounds. It obeys the same sixth-power ceiling, but its separation is quartic, from the pointer functions: they have $\lambda=\widetilde O(n^{1/4})$ against $\mathrm{D}=\widetilde\Omega(n)$, while their sensitivity is $\widetilde O(\sqrt n)$ and so gives only a quadratic gap. Sensitivity is the harder measure to hold down, and \(f_{\mathrm{BHT}}\) is the only construction that does.

Is $\mathrm{D}(f) = O(\mathrm{Q}_E(f)^2)$? total #

Gap between exponents 2 and 3.

An exact quantum algorithm has to be right on every input, which is a strong enough demand that no total function is known to gain more than a square by it. Midrijanis' simulation gives $\mathrm{D}(f)=O(\mathrm{Q}_E(f)^3)$; the pointer functions give a family with $\mathrm{D}=\widetilde\Omega(\mathrm{Q}_E^2)$.

Best known

  • \(\mathrm{D}(f) \le 2\deg(f)^3 \le 16\,\mathrm{Q}_E(f)^3\)
    Midrijanis04 BBCMdW01
    Through the degree, using $\deg\le2\mathrm{Q}_E$: an exact algorithm making $T$ queries writes the acceptance probability as a polynomial of degree $2T$, and for an exact algorithm that polynomial is $f$. Midrijanis states the cubic bound in this form; it superseded the $\mathrm{D}=O(\mathrm{Q}_E^4)$ of [BBCMdW01].
  • \(\mathrm{D}(f_n) = \widetilde\Omega\big(\mathrm{Q}_E(f_n)^2\big)\)
    witness: \(g_{\mathrm{ABBLSS}}\)ABBLSS17
    The same family that gives the quadratic $\mathrm{D}$-vs-$\mathrm{R}_0$ separation: $\mathrm{D}=\widetilde\Omega(n)$ against $\mathrm{Q}_E=\widetilde O(\sqrt n)$, so exactness costs the pointer-chasing algorithm nothing.
  • \(\mathrm{Q}_E(f) = O(\mathrm{D}(f)^{0.8675})\)
    witness: \(\mathrm{NE}^d\)Ambainis13
    Exponent $1.15$, and historically the hard part: the first proof that an exact quantum algorithm can beat a deterministic one by more than a constant at all.
  • \(\mathrm{D}(f) = O(\deg(f)^2)\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathrm{D}(f) = O(\mathrm{Q}_E(f)^2)\)
    CPS23
    And that would be tight, by the pointer functions. So this cell closes the moment $\mathrm{D}$ versus $\deg$ does.

Why it matters

$\mathrm{Q}_E$ sits between $\deg$ and $\mathrm{D}$, and this cell is pinned on both sides by open problems: $\deg\le2\mathrm{Q}_E\le2\mathrm{D}$, with Is …? asking whether the left inequality is tight and Is …? asking about the right. If $\mathrm{Q}_E=\Theta(\deg)$ then this is the $\mathrm{D}$-vs-$\deg$ cell, and the two would close together; both currently stand at $[2,3]$.

Where there is no room at all: for symmetric $f$, $\mathrm{Q}_E(f)=\Theta(n)$ [BdW02, Thm 21], so exactness costs the whole speedup.

Is $\mathrm{Q}_E(f) = O(\deg(f))$? total #

Open. Open problem 4 of [BdW02].

We know $\deg(f)\le 2\mathrm{Q}_E(f)$. Is the converse true up to constants? Equivalently: is the polynomial method tight for exact quantum algorithms?

Best known

  • \(\deg(f) \le 2\,\mathrm{Q}_E(f)\)
    BBCMdW01
  • \(\mathrm{Q}_E(f) \le \mathrm{D}(f) = O(\deg(f)^3)\)
    Midrijanis04
  • \(\mathrm{Q}_E(f) = O(\mathrm{D}(f)^{0.8675})\)
    witness: \(\mathrm{NE}^d\)Ambainis13

How much do negative weights buy the adversary? total #

Gap between exponents 2 and 6 on total functions. On partial functions the ratio is already known to be unbounded.

The positive adversary is the general one with its weights restricted to be non-negative, so $\mathrm{Adv}\le\mathrm{Adv}^{\pm}$ for free, and $\mathrm{Adv}^{\pm}=\Theta(\mathrm{Q})$ [Reichardt11]. The question is how far below the quantum query complexity the positive version can fall on a total function -- how much is lost by insisting on an adversary matrix that is entrywise non-negative.

Best known

  • \(\mathrm{Adv}^{\pm}(f) = \Theta(\mathrm{Q}(f)) \le \mathrm{D}(f) \le \mathrm{bs}(f)^3 \le \mathrm{Adv}(f)^6\)
    Reichardt11 Nisan91 Ambainis02
    Nothing here is about adversaries except the two ends: the middle is the ordinary classical chain, entered through $\mathrm{bs}\le\mathrm{Adv}^2$ [Ambainis02]. Every improvement to $\mathrm{D}$ versus $\mathrm{bs}$ lands here doubled.
  • \(\mathrm{Adv}^{\pm}(f_n) = \widetilde\Omega\big(\mathrm{Adv}(f_n)^2\big)\)
    witness: \(f_{\mathrm{CS}}\)ABK16 SpalekSzegedy06 Zhang05
    The certificate complexity barrier, $\mathrm{Adv}\le\sqrt{\mathrm{C}^0\mathrm{C}^1}$, applied to a function built to have cheap certificates and expensive quantum complexity: $\mathrm{Q}=\widetilde\Theta(n)$ against $\mathrm{C}=\widetilde O(\sqrt n)$, so $\mathrm{Adv}=\widetilde O(\sqrt n)$ whatever the truth about it is.
  • \(\mathrm{Adv}^{\pm}(g^{\circ d}) \ge \mathrm{Adv}(g^{\circ d})^{1.098}\)
    HLS07
    Smaller, but the only separation proved between the two bounds themselves rather than through $\mathrm{Q}$. Take the monotone $g$ on six bits whose minterms and maxterms are the Hamming-weight-three inputs of a function of Kushilevitz''s, so $\mathrm{C}^0(g)=\mathrm{C}^1(g)=3$; a numerically found negative-weight matrix gives $\mathrm{Adv}^{\pm}(g)\ge 2+3\sqrt5/5\approx3.341$, both bounds compose, and the barrier caps $\mathrm{Adv}(g^{\circ d})$ at $3^d$.
  • \(\sqrt{\mathrm{fbs}(f)} \le \mathrm{Adv}(f) \le \sqrt{\mathrm{FC}^0(f)\,\mathrm{FC}^1(f)} \le \mathrm{fbs}(f)\)
    Ambainis02 KulkarniTal16
    The positive adversary is pinned within a square of fractional block sensitivity, so this cell is a proxy for $\mathrm{Q}$ versus $\mathrm{fbs}$: there $\mathrm{Q}=\widetilde\Omega(\mathrm{fbs}^2)$ is known (same witness) and $\mathrm{Q}=\widetilde O(\mathrm{fbs}^2)$ would follow from $\mathrm{D}=O(\mathrm{bs}^2)$ [CPS23], which would bring the ceiling here down from $6$ to $4$.

Why it matters

$\mathrm{Adv}^{\pm}$ characterises quantum query complexity exactly, which makes it useless as an explanation: knowing $\mathrm{Q}=\Theta(\mathrm{Adv}^{\pm})$ tells you nothing you did not already have to prove. $\mathrm{Adv}$ is the version one can actually reason about, a weighting of input pairs with a combinatorial feel, and the price of that is a barrier: it never exceeds $\sqrt{\mathrm{C}^0\mathrm{C}^1}$, or even $\sqrt{\mathrm{FC}^0\mathrm{FC}^1}$ [KulkarniTal16]. This cell measures what the barrier costs.

The concrete thing that would move it: nobody has pinned $\mathrm{Adv}$ on the cheat-sheet function. It lies between $\widetilde\Omega(n^{1/4})$, from $\mathrm{fbs}\le\mathrm{Adv}^2$, and $\widetilde O(\sqrt n)$, from the barrier, against $\mathrm{Adv}^{\pm}=\widetilde\Theta(n)$ -- so the exponent that family witnesses is somewhere in $[2,4]$, and which end is right is a question about one explicit function rather than about all of them.

On partial functions the answer is as bad as it can be, and is the reason the negative-weight version was invented. For a promise problem whose $0$- and $1$-inputs are all at relative Hamming distance $\ge\epsilon$, the positive adversary cannot exceed $1/\epsilon$ -- the property-testing barrier -- so \(\mathrm{COL}_n\), where $\epsilon=1/2$, has $\mathrm{Adv}=O(1)$ against $\mathrm{Q}=\Theta(n^{1/3})$.

What is the exponent of $\deg$ versus $\mathrm{rdeg}$? total #

Gap between exponents 2 and 3. The polynomial relation itself was open from 1994 until [KKWY26] in 2026.

Fortnow's question -- is exact rational degree polynomially related to exact polynomial degree for total Boolean functions? -- was answered yes in 2026. What survives is the exponent: $\deg$ is somewhere between $\mathrm{rdeg}^2$ and $\widetilde{\mathrm{rdeg}}^3$, and $\mathrm{D}$ between $\mathrm{rdeg}^2$ and $\mathrm{rdeg}^4$.

Best known

  • \(\deg(f) = O\big(\mathrm{rdeg}(f)\deg_{\pm}(f)^2\log\mathrm{rdeg}(f)\big) = \widetilde O\big(\mathrm{rdeg}(f)^3\big)\)
    KKWY26
    Corollary 4 of [KKWY26]: the answer to Fortnow's question, and the upper end of the surviving gap.
  • \(\mathrm{D}(f) \le 4\deg_{\pm}(f)^2\mathrm{rdeg}(f)^2 \le 16\,\mathrm{rdeg}(f)^4\)
    KKWY26
    Theorem 4, which is what every other cell of the rdeg column now descends from. Its engine is minimum block sensitivity $\min_x\mathrm{bs}_x(f) \le 2\deg_{\pm}(f)^2$ -- a measure with no prior use in the literature, and one the zoo does not carry, since it is the min rather than the max over inputs.
  • \(\deg(f_n) = \Omega\big(\mathrm{rdeg}(f_n)^2\big)\)
    witness: \(\mathrm{AND}_n \circ \overline{\mathrm{EH}}_n\)IJKKKSWW23
    The lower end. On $\wedge_n\circ\overline{\mathrm{EH}}_n$, $\mathrm{rdeg}=\Theta(n)$ while $\deg=\Theta(n^2)$; the same function gives the best separation anyone has of any measure from $\mathrm{rdeg}$, namely $\widetilde{\deg} = \Omega(\mathrm{rdeg}^{3/2})$.
  • \(\mathrm{rdeg}(f) = \Omega(\deg(f)) \text{ for symmetric } f\)
    IJKKKSWW23
    Exponent $1$ on symmetric functions; the pre-2026 partial results, which are still the sharpest available on their classes.
  • \(\mathrm{rdeg}(f) = \Omega(\sqrt{\deg(f)}) \text{ for unate } f \text{ and for read-once } \mathsf{AC}^0/\mathsf{TC}^0\)
    IJKKKSWW23
  • \(\mathrm{rdeg}(f) \ge n/2 - O(\sqrt n) \text{ for almost every } f\)
    IJKKKSWW23

Why it matters

$\mathrm{rdeg}$ is the exact-error analogue of \(\mathrm{rdeg}_{\varepsilon}(f)\), and equals exact post-selected quantum query complexity [MahadevdeWolf15]. A positive answer adds a genuine new column to the classical lattice, and gives $\mathsf{P} = (\mathsf{C}_{=}\mathsf{P}\cap\mathsf{co}\text{-}\mathsf{C}_{=}\mathsf{P})$ relative to generic oracles -- which is what made Fortnow ask. The partial-function version fails in both directions, so this really is a total-function phenomenon; see \(\mathrm{GapMaj}^{\delta}_{n}\), where $\mathrm{rdeg} = \Theta(n)$ against $\mathrm{Q} = \Theta(1)$.

Two cells are filed here. $\deg$ vs $\mathrm{rdeg}$ is Fortnow's own, and sits in $[2,3]$. $\mathrm{D}$ vs $\mathrm{rdeg}$ sits in $[2,4]$ and is harder to move: the trick that removes the $\log n$ from Theorem 8 for $\deg$ and $\mathrm{s}$ is to restrict to a maximal monomial or a maximal sensitive block, which shrinks the arity without raising $\mathrm{rdeg}$. Doing the same for $\mathrm{D}$ would need decision-tree complexity to condense onto $\mathrm{poly}(\mathrm{D})$ variables, which is open. And $\mathrm{D} = O(\deg^3)$ is still the best bound by ordinary degree, so $\mathrm{D} = O(\mathrm{rdeg}^3)$ would be at least as strong as Is …? on that route.

The rest of the column -- $\mathrm{R}$, $\mathrm{C}$, $\mathrm{bs}$, $\mathrm{Q}$, $\mathrm{Q}_E$ and the others -- is no longer a separate mystery; each is an exponent gap inherited from these two.

Is $\mathrm{R}(f)$ polynomially bounded by $\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{prod}}(f)$ for total $f$? total #

Open, and wide open: no bound $\mathrm{R} \le g(\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{prod}})$ is known for any function $g$ whatsoever, not merely no polynomial one. The known separation is a single small polynomial.

Yao's minimax says $\mathrm{R}_\varepsilon(f)$ is the maximum of the distributional complexity $\mathrm{D}^{\mu}_\varepsilon(f)$ over all input distributions. Restrict the maximum to distributions in which the $n$ input bits are independent, and you get $\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{prod}}$. How much does that restriction cost?

Best known

  • \(\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{prod}}(f) \le \mathrm{R}(f)\)
    Yao77
    The trivial direction, and the only one that is known.
  • \(\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{prod}}(g_d) = O(N^{0.69789\ldots}) \text{ while } \mathrm{R}(g_d) = \Theta(N^{0.75384\ldots})\)
    Sanyal24 Santha95
    The balanced NAND tree on $N = 2^d$ leaves, a ratio of exponents of $1.08$. This is the only polynomial separation known.
  • \(\mathrm{RS}(g) = \tilde\Omega\big(\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{prod}}(g)\big)\)
    Sanyal24
    The best general handle, but it bounds $\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{prod}}$ from above, so it is the wrong way round to close the cell -- and the NAND tree shows it is not tight.

Why it matters

The motivation is composition. $\mathrm{R}(f \circ g^n) = \tilde\Omega(\mathrm{R}(f)\, \mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{prod}}(g))$ [Sanyal24], so a positive answer here would come close to the randomized composition conjecture from a direction independent of sabotage complexity (Is … for total …?). It is also the cleanest quantitative form of a question with a long history: how much of the hardness of a Boolean function lives in correlations between its input bits, rather than in the bits themselves?

What makes the NAND tree separate is worth internalising, because it is not the usual "product distributions are too weak" hand-wave. Saks and Wigderson's classic $\Theta(\alpha^d)$ bound, $\alpha = (1+\sqrt{33})/4$, is proved against a product distribution -- but at zero error. Under a product distribution the two subtrees at a gate are independent, so an algorithm that knows $\mu$ can always recurse first on the child likelier to evaluate to $0$ instead of on a random one, and that greedy choice compounds over the depth: the recursion closes at $\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{27}(17+7\sqrt7)} = 1.62207\ldots$ per level rather than $\alpha = 1.68614\ldots$. Against a correlated distribution the choice is not available. So the separation is powered by independence itself, which suggests that any counterexample to the polynomial relation will have to be deep rather than wide -- on the depth-$2$ \(\mathrm{MP}_m\) tree the same trick buys only a logarithmic factor.

How hard is it to solve $k$ copies? relation #

Direct sum settled up to constants for $\mathrm{D}$ (exactly) and $\mathrm{R}$; the sharp constants, the large-budget direct product, and the quantum case are open.

Three related questions about $f^{\otimes k}$, all raised in the papers that settled the easy parts.

  1. Worst-case direct sum, sharply. [JainKlauckSantha10] prove $\mathrm{R}_\varepsilon(f^{\otimes k}) \ge \delta^2 k\,\mathrm{R}_{\varepsilon/(1-\delta)+\delta}(f)$ for every relation $f$. The $\delta^2$ loss and the additive $\delta$ in the error are known to be necessary in the distributional setting ([Shaltiel04]) -- is either necessary in the worst case?
  2. Direct product above the threshold. [Drucker12] gives a DPT with query budget $\gamma^3\mathrm{R}(f)k/11$. What worst-case success probability is achievable with $\alpha\,\mathrm{R}(f)k$ queries for $\alpha \gg 1$? Quantumly this was settled by [BNRdW07]; classically it is open, and the interesting regime is exactly the one where the $O(\mathrm{R}(f)k\log k)$ upper bound lives.
  3. A tight quantum direct sum. Is $\mathrm{Q}(f^{\otimes k}) = \Theta(k\,\mathrm{Q}(f))$? The known bound loses a log factor and holds only for Boolean functions, because it goes through the adversary bound; [JainKlauckSantha10] ask for a direct proof, even for relations.

The Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture total #

Proved in November 2026 by David Turturean at Physical Superintelligence (PSI).

Every bounded degree-$d$ polynomial $p:\{-1,1\}^n\to[-1,1]$ has a variable of influence at least $c\,\mathrm{Var}(p)^{5/2}/d^6$, for an absolute $c>0$. PSI/Turturean proves the Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture [PSI26AaronsonAmbainis, Turturean26]. Cleaner expositions are available in [BDST26AA] and [HMP26AA].

Best known

  • \(\max_i\mathrm{Inf}_i(p)\ge c\,\mathrm{Var}(p)^{5/2}/d^6\)
    PSI26AaronsonAmbainis Turturean26
    Influence normalization is as in the paper. Cleaner expositions: [BDST26AA] (arXiv:2611.11626) and [HMP26AA] (arXiv:2611.12795).

Why it matters

Quantum query algorithms can be simulated with polynomially many classical queries on most uniformly random inputs. For a partial Boolean function whose domain has density $\delta$, [HMP26AA] obtains a deterministic $O(\mathrm{Q}(f)^6/(\varepsilon\delta)^2)$-query algorithm correct on at least a $1-\varepsilon$ fraction of its domain. This is an average-case guarantee.

Does randomized query complexity compose for total functions? total #

Solved up to polylogarithmic factors in n (outer input size), not R(f); some slack is necessary.

Randomized composition holds with a $\log(n+1)$ loss, even for partial Boolean functions [PednekarPodder26STOC]. The polylogarithmic loss is in the outer input size $n$, not in $\mathrm{R}(f)$; the theorem does not guarantee a $\log(\mathrm{R}(f)+1)$ loss.

Meiburg's work shows that some polylogarithmic slack is necessary, even for total functions [Meiburg26composition]. His families save a factor $\Theta(\log\mathrm{R}(g))=\Theta(\log\log L)$, where $L$ is the composed input length, ruling out constant-factor multiplicative composition.

    The logarithm is of the outer input length n, not R(f). Both f and g may be partial Boolean functions.

Composition remains available for building query lower bounds with a logarithmic loss in outer input size. Meiburg's total-function counterexample shows that some polylogarithmic slack is necessary.
